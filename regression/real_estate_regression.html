5. Regression Analysis

5.1 Introduction

Regression analysis is a statistical and machine learning technique used to examine the relationship between a continuous dependent variable and one or more independent variables. In this analysis, Multiple Linear Regression is used to investigate the factors associated with residential property prices.

The Real Estate Valuation dataset contains information about property transactions, including transaction date, house age, distance to the nearest Mass Rapid Transit (MRT) station, number of nearby convenience stores, geographical coordinates, and the corresponding house price per unit area.

The objective of this analysis is to determine how these property and location-related characteristics are associated with house prices and to develop a regression model capable of predicting house price per unit area for previously unseen observations.

Unlike the classification analysis, where the target variable represented discrete classes, the response variable in this analysis is continuous. Therefore, regression techniques are appropriate for this dataset.

The analysis follows a structured modelling process. First, the dataset is explored using descriptive statistics and visualisations. The data is then divided into training and testing subsets to allow the final model to be evaluated on observations that were not used during model development. Multiple Linear Regression is subsequently fitted using the training data. The statistical significance of the predictors and the overall model performance are examined. An alternative regression model is then considered for comparison, followed by residual diagnostics to assess the assumptions of the regression model. Finally, the selected model is evaluated using the independent test dataset.

5.2 Dataset Description

The Real Estate Valuation dataset contains observations of residential property transactions. The response variable is the house price of unit area, while six explanatory variables describe the transaction, property characteristics, accessibility and geographical location.

Variable Description Role
X1 Transaction date Predictor
X2 House age Predictor
X3 Distance to nearest MRT station Predictor
X4 Number of convenience stores Predictor
X5 Latitude Predictor
X6 Longitude Predictor
Y House price of unit area Response

The response variable, house price of unit area, is continuous and therefore suitable for regression modelling. The six predictor variables provide information about the temporal, physical, accessibility and geographical characteristics of the properties.

The dataset will first be examined for its structure, missing observations and descriptive characteristics before the regression models are developed.

Multiple Linear Regression

Multiple linear regression was performed to examine the relationship between house price and the explanatory variables in the dataset. The dependent variable is house_price, while transaction_date, house_age, mrt_distance, convenience_stores, latitude, and longitude are used as predictor variables.

regression_model <- lm(
  house_price ~ transaction_date + house_age + mrt_distance +
    convenience_stores + latitude + longitude,
  data = real_estate
)

Interpretation of the Multiple Linear Regression Model

The multiple linear regression model was statistically significant, with an F-statistic of 94.6 and a p-value of less than 2.2 × 10⁻¹⁶. This indicates that the set of explanatory variables, taken together, provides a statistically significant explanation of variation in house prices.

The model produced an R-squared value of 0.5824, meaning that approximately 58.24% of the variation in house prices is explained by the variables included in the model. The adjusted R-squared value was 0.5762, indicating that approximately 57.62% of the variation is explained after accounting for the number of predictors in the model.

The regression results show that transaction_date, house_age, mrt_distance, convenience_stores, and latitude are statistically significant predictors of house price at the 5% significance level. In contrast, longitude is not statistically significant (p = 0.7982) after controlling for the other variables.

The coefficient for transaction_date is positive (5.149), indicating that, holding the other variables constant, an increase of one unit in transaction date is associated with an estimated increase of approximately 5.15 units in house price.

The coefficient for house_age is negative (-0.270), suggesting that, holding other variables constant, a one-unit increase in house age is associated with an estimated decrease of approximately 0.27 units in house price.

mrt_distance also has a negative coefficient (-0.00449), indicating that properties located farther from an MRT station tend to have lower house prices, when the other predictors are held constant.

convenience_stores has a positive coefficient (1.133), suggesting that properties with a greater number of nearby convenience stores tend to have higher house prices, controlling for the other variables.

The coefficient for latitude is positive (225.5) and statistically significant, indicating a positive association between latitude and house price after controlling for the other explanatory variables.

Although longitude showed a moderate correlation with house price in the correlation analysis, it is not statistically significant in the multiple regression model. This demonstrates that the effect of a predictor can change after controlling for other variables and highlights the importance of considering predictors jointly rather than relying only on pairwise correlations.

Regression Diagnostic Assessment

The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess the normality of the regression residuals. The test produced a W statistic of 0.8762 with a p-value below 2.2 × 10⁻¹⁶. Therefore, the null hypothesis of normally distributed residuals is rejected at the 5% significance level, indicating evidence of deviation from normality. However, the dataset contains 414 observations, and the Shapiro-Wilk test can be sensitive to relatively small departures from normality in larger samples. Therefore, the Q-Q diagnostic plot should also be considered when assessing the practical severity of the violation.

Cook’s distance was examined to identify potentially influential observations. Using the screening criterion of 4/n, where n = 414, 19 observations were flagged as potentially influential. The maximum Cook’s distance was approximately 0.195. These observations were not automatically removed because exceeding the screening threshold does not necessarily indicate that an observation is erroneous. Further investigation is required before deciding whether any observations should be excluded from the analysis.

Test for Homoscedasticity

The studentized Breusch-Pagan test was conducted to assess whether the regression residuals have constant variance. The test produced a BP statistic of 8.4674 with 6 degrees of freedom and a p-value of 0.2058.

Since the p-value is greater than the 5% significance level, there is insufficient evidence to reject the null hypothesis of constant variance. Therefore, the results do not indicate a significant heteroscedasticity problem, and the homoscedasticity assumption is considered reasonable for the fitted regression model.

Investigation of Influential Observations

Cook’s distance identified several observations that exceeded the screening threshold of 4/n. Observation 271 had the largest Cook’s distance (0.1949), substantially higher than the other observations examined. This observation was therefore investigated further.

Observation 271 has a house age of 10.8 years, an MRT distance of approximately 253 metres, one nearby convenience store, and a house price of 117.5, which is the maximum house price in the dataset. Its predictor values fall within the observed ranges of the dataset, and there was no evidence of an obvious data-entry error.

Therefore, observation 271 was retained in the dataset. The presence of influential observations was acknowledged rather than removing valid observations solely to improve model diagnostics.

Model Performance

The predictive performance of the multiple linear regression model was assessed using the Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), Mean Absolute Error (MAE), and Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE).

actual <- real_estate$house_price
predicted <- fitted(regression_model)

rmse <- sqrt(mean((actual - predicted)^2))
mae <- mean(abs(actual - predicted))
mape <- mean(abs((actual - predicted) / actual)) * 100

model_performance <- data.frame(

  MAE = mae,

)

round(model_performance, 3)

Interpretation of Model Performance

The fitted regression model was evaluated using RMSE, MAE, and MAPE. The model produced an RMSE of 8.782, an MAE of 6.131, and a MAPE of 18.621%.

The MAE indicates that the model’s predicted house prices differ from the observed values by approximately 6.13 units on average. The RMSE is higher than the MAE because it places greater emphasis on larger prediction errors, suggesting that some observations have comparatively larger residuals.

The MAPE indicates an average absolute percentage error of approximately 18.62% for the observations used to fit the model. These metrics represent the model’s performance on the training data and should therefore not be interpreted as an estimate of performance on unseen data.

Conclusion

This analysis investigated the factors associated with house prices using the Real Estate Valuation dataset and developed a multiple linear regression model for predicting house prices. The exploratory analysis showed that several property and location-related variables were associated with house price. In particular, MRT distance showed a relatively strong negative correlation with house price, while the number of convenience stores showed a positive correlation. House age also showed a negative relationship with house price.

A multiple linear regression model was then fitted using transaction date, house age, MRT distance, number of convenience stores, latitude, and longitude as explanatory variables. The overall regression model was statistically significant (p < 2.2 × 10⁻¹⁶) and explained approximately 58.2% of the variation in house prices based on the full dataset. Transaction date, house age, MRT distance, convenience stores, and latitude were statistically significant predictors, while longitude was not statistically significant.

Several regression assumptions and diagnostic checks were performed. The variance inflation factors did not indicate severe multicollinearity, with the largest VIF being approximately 4.32. The Breusch-Pagan test did not provide significant evidence of heteroscedasticity (p = 0.2058). However, the Shapiro-Wilk test indicated that the residuals did not follow a normal distribution (p < 2.2 × 10⁻¹⁶). Influential observations were also investigated using Cook’s distance. Although several observations exceeded the screening threshold, the observations were retained because there was no evidence that they represented invalid or erroneous records.

The predictive performance of the model was evaluated using an 80/20 training-testing split. On the unseen testing data, the full model achieved an RMSE of 7.179, an MAE of 5.603, and a MAPE of 15.337%. The actual versus predicted plot also showed that most predictions were reasonably close to the diagonal reference line, providing visual support for the model’s predictive ability.

Model tuning was performed by comparing the full model with a reduced model that excluded longitude. The reduced model had a slightly higher adjusted R-squared and a lower AIC, indicating a small improvement in model parsimony. However, the full model produced marginally lower RMSE, MAE, and MAPE values on the unseen testing data. Therefore, the full model was selected as the final predictive model.

Overall, the analysis demonstrates that multiple linear regression can provide a useful approach for explaining and predicting house prices in this dataset. The model shows reasonable predictive performance and generalisability to the selected testing sample, although the non-normality of the residuals and the remaining prediction errors indicate that there is scope for further improvement. Additional variables, nonlinear relationships, interaction effects, or alternative machine learning models could potentially improve predictive accuracy in future analyses.

Reflection

Reflection on the Analysis

This analysis provided an opportunity to apply the concepts of exploratory data analysis, correlation analysis, multiple linear regression, model diagnostics, and predictive evaluation to a real estate dataset. One of the main successes was developing a complete analytical workflow rather than focusing only on fitting a regression model. The analysis progressed from understanding the dataset and identifying relationships between variables to evaluating regression assumptions and testing the model on unseen data.

One of the challenges during the analysis was interpreting the different statistical results and understanding how they affected the regression model. For example, the correlation analysis showed strong relationships between some variables, particularly MRT distance and house price. However, correlation alone does not determine whether a variable remains statistically significant when other predictors are included in a multiple regression model. This highlighted the importance of considering both exploratory relationships and regression results when drawing conclusions.

Another challenge was evaluating the assumptions of linear regression. The diagnostic analysis indicated that the residuals did not follow a normal distribution according to the Shapiro-Wilk test. However, the Breusch-Pagan test did not provide significant evidence of heteroscedasticity. These results demonstrated that regression assumptions should be evaluated individually rather than assuming that a model either completely satisfies or violates all assumptions.

The analysis also involved investigating potentially influential observations using Cook’s distance. Rather than automatically removing observations that exceeded the screening threshold, the influential observations were examined in the context of the dataset. Observation 271, for example, had a relatively large Cook’s distance but represented a valid observation within the observed range of the dataset. This led to the decision to retain the observations rather than remove them without sufficient justification.

Model generalisability was another important consideration. Instead of evaluating the model only on the data used for training, the dataset was divided into training and testing subsets. The final model achieved a test RMSE of 7.179, MAE of 5.603, and MAPE of 15.337%. This provided evidence of how the model performed on observations that were not used during model fitting.

Model tuning was performed by comparing the original full model with a reduced model that excluded longitude because it was not statistically significant. The reduced model had a slightly lower AIC and marginally higher adjusted R-squared, but the full model produced slightly better predictive performance on the testing dataset. This demonstrated the importance of considering both model simplicity and predictive performance when selecting a final model.

Future Work

There are several ways in which this analysis could be extended. First, nonlinear relationships and interaction effects could be investigated. For example, the relationship between MRT distance and house price may not be completely linear, and the effect of location-related variables could potentially depend on other characteristics of the property.

Second, alternative predictive approaches such as decision trees, random forests, or other machine learning regression methods could be compared with multiple linear regression. This could determine whether more flexible models provide improved predictive accuracy.

A further extension would be to use repeated cross-validation rather than relying on a single training-testing split. This would provide a more robust estimate of the model’s generalisation performance across different subsets of the data.

Finally, additional property and location variables could be incorporated if available. Variables such as property size, floor level, building condition, or additional measures of accessibility may provide further information and potentially improve the prediction of house prices.
